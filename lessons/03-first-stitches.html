Lesson 03 · The core skills

Slip knot & casting on

Casting on means creating the first row of loops out of nothing. It is the only part of knitting where you are not working into existing stitches — and it is where almost everyone's first attempt goes wrong, in one of exactly two ways. Both are covered below.

25 minutes Yarn + one needle Practise on 20 stitches before the real thing

First, the slip knot

A slip knot is an adjustable loop. It becomes your very first stitch. It is the only actual knot in the whole garment.

How to tell you did it right Pull the tail end and the loop should tighten. Pull the working end (the one going to the ball) and the whole knot should slide along the yarn. If pulling either end just makes a fixed knot that will not adjust, undo it and try again — you have tied a plain overhand knot.

The long-tail cast on

This is the cast on to learn. It is fast, it produces a neat elastic edge, and it creates the first row of knitting at the same time as casting on — so when you finish, you are already one row in.

It uses two strands at once: the working yarn from the ball, and a long tail you measured out first. That is why it is called long-tail, and why the first question is always “how long?”

How long a tail?

The classic rule is about 2.5 cm of tail per stitch for DK yarn, plus 15 cm spare. Or wrap the yarn around the needle the number of times you need stitches and use that length. Or — the method that never fails — use both ends of a centre-pull ball and never run out.

Mistake 1 · casting on too tightly The commonest problem in the whole craft. A tight cast-on edge will not stretch over a dog's head, and the first row is agony to knit. Fix: cast on over two needles held together, then slide one out. Or simply use a needle two sizes larger for the cast-on row only.
Mistake 2 · running out of tail You get to stitch 68 of 76 and the tail is 4 cm long. There is no fix but to undo it all. Measure generously — a wasted 40 cm of yarn costs nothing, and re-casting-on 76 stitches costs twenty minutes and your good mood.

The knitted cast on — a gentler alternative

If the long-tail slingshot is not clicking, this one uses only skills you already have, needs no measured tail, and is impossible to run out of. It is slightly less elastic, so work it loosely.

  1. Make a slip knot and put it on the left needle.

    That is stitch one.

  2. Knit into it — but do not slide it off.

    Insert the right needle, wrap, pull a loop through. Stop there. You now have a new loop on the right needle and the old stitch still on the left.

  3. Move the new loop onto the left needle.

    Slip it from the right needle tip onto the left needle tip, without twisting it. You now have two stitches on the left needle.

  4. Repeat into the newest stitch.

    Knit into the stitch you just made, transfer the new loop across, and keep going until you have the number you need.

You will not know how to “knit into it” until lesson 4 — so if you are reading in order, come back to this after the next lesson. That is fine and expected.

Counting what you cast on

Seventy-six stitches is a lot to count on a wobbly needle. Two habits make it painless:

Mark every 20

Drop a stitch marker onto the needle after every 20th stitch as you cast on. Then counting is “three markers and eleven more” instead of counting 71 loops one at a time. Remove them on the first round, or leave them — they do no harm.

Count in the same direction, twice

Count once from the tip towards the cable, then again from the cable to the tip. If the two numbers disagree, count a third time before adding or removing anything.

Practise this, not the sweater

Before you cast on for the real thing:

Twenty stitches takes about two minutes once it clicks. It is worth doing three times.
