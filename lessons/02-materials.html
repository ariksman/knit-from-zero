Yarn, needles & tools

There is an enormous amount of knitting equipment in the world and you need about six things. This lesson tells you which six, why, and what the label on a ball of yarn is actually telling you.

15 minutes Shopping list at the end Budget: roughly €25–45 for everything

Yarn weight is about thickness, not mass

“Weight” in knitting means how thick the strand is. It runs from lace (thread) to jumbo (rope). Thicker yarn needs bigger needles, makes bigger stitches, and finishes faster — but it also makes stiffer fabric, and a dog has to be able to bend at the shoulder.

For this project the answer is DK (also sold as light worsted or 8-ply). It is fast enough to finish in a few evenings, thin enough to move in, and it is the most widely available weight in machine-washable wool.

This matters more than usual here, because the garment is going to be worn by an animal that rolls in things and cannot be told to hand-wash itself.

A Basenji-specific note Basenjis have a single, very short coat and no undercoat. They genuinely get cold below about 10 °C and they let you know about it. They are also fastidious — a scratchy yarn will be rejected instantly and thoroughly. Soft matters here in a way it does not for a Husky's novelty jumper. Rub the yarn against the inside of your wrist in the shop; if it prickles you, it will prickle the dog.

Reading a ball band

Every ball has a paper band, and every band tells you the same six things. Once you can read one, you can shop anywhere in the world.

The gauge square

A little grid showing how many stitches and rows the manufacturer got over 10 cm, and on what needle. Treat it as the starting point for your swatch, not as a promise. Your hands are not their hands.

The dye lot

A batch number. Two balls of the same colour from different lots can differ enough to show as a visible stripe across the finished sweater. Buy all your yarn at once, from the same lot, and buy one ball more than you think you need.

Needles

Knitting needles come in three shapes, and for this project you want the third.

Buy circulars, even for flat knitting A 40 cm circular needle can do everything a pair of straights can do — you just turn at the end of the row instead of going round. The reverse is not true. One circular in each size is a complete kit.

Material

Bamboo / wood

Slightly grippy, so stitches do not slide off while you are still clumsy. Warm to hold. Best for a first project.

Metal

Fast and slippery. Wonderful once you are confident, treacherous on day one. Sharp tips are good for tight stitches.

Plastic / resin

Cheap and light. Cables on cheap circulars are often stiff and coiled, which is genuinely annoying — check that the cable is soft.

The five other things

Stitch markers

Little rings that sit on the needle between stitches to mark a position. You need three: one distinctive one for the start of the round and two for the sides. A loop of contrasting yarn works perfectly if you have none.

Tapestry needle

A blunt, large-eyed needle for weaving in ends. Blunt is important — a sharp one splits the yarn and makes a mess. Under €2.

Soft tape measure

The dressmaker's kind, not a builder's tape. You need it for your dog and for your gauge swatch.

Scissors

Any small sharp pair.

Scrap yarn or a stitch holder

For parking stitches you are not working on. Smooth cotton scrap yarn is better than a rigid holder — it bends.

Wool wash (optional)

A no-rinse detergent for blocking. A capful of hair conditioner in cool water does the same job.

Do not buy a “beginner kit” They are usually a bundle of straight needles in sizes you will never use, plus acrylic yarn in a colour nobody chose. Buy the six things above instead; it costs less and all of it gets used.
