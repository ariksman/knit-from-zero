Fabric & gauge

Four fabrics from two stitches, and then the single most important twenty minutes of the whole project: the gauge swatch. Skip this lesson and the sweater will fit some other dog.

35 minutes + drying time Do not skip the swatch Calculator included

Four fabrics, two stitches

The only decision you ever make is “knit or purl” — but where you make that decision changes everything about how the fabric behaves.

GarterKnit every row.

Flat, squashy, reversible, and it does not curl. Stretches vertically more than sideways. Used for the belly panel.

StockinetteKnit RS, purl WS.

Smooth and even, the default garment fabric. Curls hard at every edge, so it always needs a border. Used for the body.

2×2 ribk2, p2, repeat.

Pulls in hard sideways and springs back. This is what grips a dog's neck and waist. Used for the collar and hem.

Seed / mossAlternate every stitch and every row.

Thick, flat, no curl, no stretch. Slow to knit. Lovely as an edging if you want something less sporty than rib.

Why stockinette curls and rib does not

A knit stitch and a purl stitch are not the same size. The purl bump takes up more depth, so the purl face of the fabric wants to be slightly bigger than the knit face. In stockinette every stitch on one side is a knit and every stitch on the other is a purl, so the whole sheet bends — top and bottom roll towards the knit side, the sides roll towards the purl side.

In rib, knit and purl columns alternate, so those forces cancel out along the row. Better than that: the purl columns sink behind the knit columns, so the fabric folds up like a concertina and can open out again. That is where ribbing's grip comes from — it is not elastic yarn, it is folded geometry.

See it in three dimensions

Everything above is true but flat. Here is the same fabric as real geometry — one continuous strand of yarn, thickened and lit. Switch between the four fabrics and turn the model over.

Drag “relax the rib” Choose 2×2 rib first. Nothing about the stitches changes as you drag — the fabric simply folds, and the same width of knitting takes up less room. That is the entire mechanism behind ribbing, and no flat picture can show it.
Drag “let the swatch go” Choose stockinette. The top and bottom edges roll one way, the sides the other. Now you can see why a swatch measured while curling gives a nonsense number, and why every edge on the sweater is ribbing or garter.

Gauge: the number the whole project hangs on

Gauge (British knitters say tension) is how many stitches and rows you get per 10 cm. It depends on the yarn, the needles, and — more than either of those — on you. Two people with identical yarn and needles routinely differ by three stitches over 10 cm, which is a 14% difference in size.

What 10% too loose looks like Suppose the pattern expects 22 sts per 10 cm and you actually get 20. Casting on the pattern's 120 stitches then gives you 120 ÷ 2.0 = 60 cm of chest instead of 54.5 cm. That is a whole size out — a loose, sliding sweater that catches on things. In the other direction, at 24 sts per 10 cm you would get 50 cm, and it would not go on at all.

You have two ways to deal with this, and both are legitimate:

1 · Change your needles to match the pattern

Too many stitches per 10 cm (too tight)? Go up a needle size. Too few (too loose)? Go down. Re-swatch. Repeat until you match. This is what you do when following a published pattern that gives one set of numbers.

2 · Change the pattern to match you ★

Measure your actual gauge and recalculate every stitch count from it. Historically this meant arithmetic; here the generator does it. Knit at whatever tension is comfortable, tell it the number, and it rewrites the pattern.

How to make a swatch that does not lie

  1. Cast on enough stitches for about 15 cm.

    For DK that is roughly 34 stitches. Bigger than the 10 cm you are going to measure — the edges of a swatch are always distorted and you must be able to avoid them.

  2. Work a border, then the real fabric, then a border.

    Five rows of garter, then stockinette with 4 garter stitches at each edge, then five rows of garter. The border stops it curling into a tube you cannot measure.

  3. Make it at least 15 cm tall, then bind off loosely.

    Row gauge needs as much room as stitch gauge.

  4. Wash it exactly as you will wash the sweater.

    This is the step everyone skips and it is the one that matters most. Superwash merino in particular relaxes and grows when washed — sometimes by 5–10%. A swatch measured dry off the needles tells you about a garment that will not exist after its first wash.

  5. Dry it flat, unstretched, then measure across the middle.

    Lay a ruler down and count how many stitches lie in 10 cm. Count half stitches honestly. Then do the same vertically for rows. Measure in three different places and average them.

Swatch in the round if you will knit in the round Most knitters purl at a slightly different tension than they knit. Since the sweater body is worked in the round — all knit, no purl — a flat swatch can be misleading. If you want to be thorough, work your swatch as a small tube on the circular needle. If that feels like too much, knit the flat swatch and accept ±3% of error; the fit tolerance of a dog sweater absorbs it.

Row gauge matters less — until it doesn't

Stitch gauge controls circumference and is unforgiving. Row gauge controls length, and most patterns work around it by saying “knit until the piece measures 20 cm” rather than “knit 60 rows”.

But it does matter in one place in this project: the leg openings. The back panel and the belly panel are worked separately for the same number of rows and then rejoined. If your row gauge is far off, the openings will be the wrong depth — too shallow and they bind at the shoulder, too deep and the sweater gapes. The generator sizes them in rows from your row gauge, which is why it asks for it.

Before you move on

Write your two numbers down somewhere. You will type them into the pattern generator and they will control every stitch count in the project.
