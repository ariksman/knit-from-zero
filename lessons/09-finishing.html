Lesson 09 · The core skills

Finishing & blocking

The last hour of a project is the one that decides whether it looks handmade or homemade. Weaving in, picking up, and blocking are all quick — and all of them are routinely skipped, which is why so much hand-knitting looks slightly sad.

25 minutes + drying Tapestry needle required Do not skip the blocking

Weaving in ends

Every time you join a new ball or bind off you get a tail. On a jumper for a person you can be fairly relaxed about these. On something a dog will wear, roll on, scratch at and shove through a hedge, you cannot — a tail that works loose will unravel a stitch, and a loose loop of yarn around a claw is a genuine hazard.

The method below is called duplicate stitch weaving. It traces the path of the existing stitches with the tail, so the tail is locked in by friction along its whole length rather than just knotted at one point.

  1. Thread the tail on a blunt tapestry needle.

    Work on the wrong side, with the fabric flat.

  2. Follow a column of stitches for about 5 cm.

    Slide the needle under the back of each stitch in turn — under the yarn, never through it. The tail should disappear into the fabric, not sit on top of it.

  3. Turn around and go back 2 cm.

    Along the neighbouring column, in the opposite direction. This is the step that makes it permanent.

  4. Stretch the fabric in both directions, then snip.

    Stretching first lets the tail settle. Cut it flush; do not leave a stub “just in case”, and never tie a knot — knots create a hard lump that rubs.

On a dog garment, weave longer than feels necessary Five centimetres forward and two back, minimum, for every end. It takes an extra thirty seconds each and it is the difference between a sweater that survives two winters and one that starts shedding stitches in March.

Picking up stitches

Picking up means creating new live stitches along an existing edge, so you can knit outwards from it. In this project you use it to put a small ribbed cuff around each leg opening — optional, but it stops the openings stretching and gives the sweater a much more finished look.

Spacing without counting every gap Divide the edge in half with a marker, then each half in half again. Work out how many stitches belong in each quarter, and pick up that many in each. Any unevenness is spread out instead of accumulating at the end.

Seaming (which you barely need)

The sweater in this course is knitted in one piece, in the round, so it has no seams at all. But mattress stitch is worth knowing in ten lines, because you will meet it in the first flat pattern you ever try.

Mattress stitch, on stockinette: lay the two pieces side by side, right sides up, edges touching. Between the outermost stitch and the next one in, you will see a ladder of small horizontal bars. Pick up one bar from the left piece, then the matching bar from the right piece, alternating, and pull gently every few stitches. The seam pulls closed and becomes invisible from the front. It uses one full stitch from each edge, which is why patterns tell you to add “selvedge stitches”.

Blocking: what it actually does

Blocking is washing the finished garment and drying it in the shape you want. It sounds like an optional flourish. It is not. When wool is wet, the fibres relax and release the tension your hands put into them; as it dries in position, it sets that way.

It evens out the stitches

Every knitter's tension varies over an evening. Blocking blurs those differences into an even fabric.

It sets the measurements

A garment can be persuaded 2–3 cm in either direction while wet. This is your one chance to correct a slightly-too-small chest.

It flattens curl

Stockinette edges that roll stubbornly will lie flat after blocking — mostly. Ribbing is deliberately left to spring back.

  1. Fill a basin with cool water and a little wool wash.

    No-rinse wool wash, or a capful of hair conditioner. Cool, not warm — sudden temperature changes are what felts wool.

  2. Submerge the sweater and leave it for 20 minutes.

    Push it under gently. Do not agitate, swirl or rub. Wool needs time to wet through, and agitation plus heat is exactly the recipe for felting.

  3. Lift it out supporting the whole weight, and squeeze.

    Never wring, and never let it hang — wet wool is heavy and will stretch permanently under its own weight.

  4. Roll it in a dry towel and stand on it.

    Genuinely. Lay the sweater on a towel, roll the towel up like a Swiss roll, and press down on it. This removes far more water than squeezing.

  5. Lay it flat and pat it out to the finished measurements.

    On a dry towel, or a spare bed. Pat the chest out to the number your pattern gave you, straighten the leg openings into rectangles, square up the hem. Leave the ribbing relaxed — do not stretch the collar out or you will lose its grip.

  6. Leave it until it is completely dry.

    A day, maybe two. Turn it over halfway. Do not put it on the dog while it is even slightly damp.

Reinforcing the parts that will wear

Three places on a dog sweater take almost all the abrasion. Two minutes each now saves a repair later.

PlaceWhy it wearsWhat to do
The two front corners of each leg openingEvery step pulls at them.Work a few duplicate stitches over each corner on the wrong side, using the tail you already have there.
The collar edgePulled over the head repeatedly, and chewed by the wearer's friends.Make sure the bind-off is stretchy but not loose, and weave that end especially long.
The centre of the backWhere a harness or a lead clip sits.If your dog wears a harness over the sweater, consider a few rows of doubled yarn there, or simply accept it as the first place to darn.

Care label for the finished sweater

Wash: machine, 30 °C, wool or delicate cycle, inside a pillowcase or mesh bag
Spin: low, or not at all
Dry: flat, out of direct sun and off the radiator
Never: tumble dryer, bleach, fabric softener
Between washes: air it. Wool is self-cleaning to a surprising degree and washing it every week wears it out faster than the dog does.

All of the above assumes superwash yarn. If you used untreated wool, everything becomes hand-wash-only — cool water, no agitation, same drying method.
