Lesson 08 · The core skills

Fixing mistakes

Knitting is one continuous strand, which means almost anything can be undone and redone. The skill is not avoiding mistakes — you will make plenty — it is noticing them early and knowing which repair to reach for.

25 minutes Read before you need it Keep a crochet hook nearby

Insurance first: the lifeline

A lifeline is a length of smooth contrasting yarn threaded through every live stitch of a row. If everything goes wrong above it, you rip back to the lifeline and every stitch is sitting there, held, in the right order, none of them twisted.

Thread one every time you finish a section of the sweater — after the collar, after the chest increases, after the leg openings. It costs two minutes and it converts a possible disaster into a possible inconvenience.

How to put in a lifeline

  1. Cut a metre of smooth cotton yarn or dental floss, in a colour you would never knit with.
  2. Thread it on a tapestry needle.
  3. Slide the needle through every stitch on the circular needle, following the needle exactly — do not go through the stitch markers, and do not split the yarn.
  4. Leave the ends hanging loose. Keep knitting; the lifeline just sits in the fabric.

The dropped stitch

A stitch slips off the needle and the column below it unzips into a ladder. This looks catastrophic and takes about ninety seconds to fix.

  1. Stop and secure it.

    Put a safety pin, a stitch marker or a spare needle through the loose loop immediately, before it drops any further. Then look at the damage in good light.

  2. Insert a crochet hook into the loose loop, from the front.

    Front to back, the same direction the right needle goes when you knit. A hook one size smaller than your needle is easiest.

  3. Catch the lowest rung and pull it through the loop.

    The loop is now one row higher, and the rung has become a stitch. Repeat for each rung in turn, working upwards.

  4. Put the last loop back on the left needle, not twisted.

    Its right leg should sit at the front of the needle, like all its neighbours. Compare it with the stitch next door before you carry on.

If the dropped stitch is a purl On the purl side, hook the rung through from the back instead of the front. If you get it the wrong way round, the stitch will sit as a knit in a field of purls and you will see it instantly — just drop it again and redo that one rung.

The twisted stitch

A stitch whose legs are crossed at the base. It happens when a stitch is put back on the needle the wrong way round, or worked through the back loop by accident. It is tighter than its neighbours and sits at a slight angle.

Spotting it

Every stitch on the needle should have its right leg in front of the needle and its left leg behind. Run your eye along the needle before each round; a twisted one will look like a little X at the base instead of a smooth U.

Fixing it

If it is still on the needle: slip it off, turn it around, put it back. If it is one row down: knit up to it, drop it deliberately one row, and re-hook it the right way with a crochet hook. If it is ten rows down in the middle of the body — honestly, leave it. Nobody will find it, least of all the dog.

Undoing work on purpose

Tinking — one stitch at a time

“Tink” is “knit” backwards, which is the whole joke and the whole method. Put the left needle into the stitch below the one on the right needle, front to back, slip the right stitch off, and pull the yarn free. Repeat.

Use it for: up to about a row. It is slow but completely safe — nothing can run.

Frogging — ripping back

Slide everything off the needle and pull. Called frogging because you “rip it, rip it”.

Use it for: more than a row or two. Rip to one row above where you want to be, then tink the final row so you can put each stitch back on the needle facing the right way. If you have a lifeline, rip straight down to it instead.

Putting stitches back on the needle Always pick them up with the tip pointing the same way you will knit, and check the leg direction as you go. A whole row put back twisted is a bigger problem than the one you were fixing. Going in with the needle from behind is usually right for stockinette — but look at an untouched stitch and copy it rather than trusting a rule.

One stitch too fewTwo stitches worked together by accident, or one slipped off the end of the needle several rows ago.Look for a ladder first. If there is none, work a M1 on the next round to restore the count — one extra increase in a dog sweater is invisible.
Count drifts by severalThe stitch markers have moved, or you have been counting the marker as a stitch.Recount in groups of ten, moving each group past a finger. Markers are never stitches.

When to leave it alone

A rule that will save you many evenings Hold the work at arm's length in normal light. If you cannot see the mistake, it is not a mistake. A dog sweater is going to be worn by something that rolls in mud. One slightly loose stitch on the left flank is not a defect, it is a feature that proves a human made it.

The things genuinely worth ripping back for are: a wrong stitch count (it compounds), a twist in the join (unfixable, see lesson 7), shaping worked in the wrong place (it changes the fit), and a split stitch (it will break). Everything else is optional.

Practice the repair before you need it

Take your practice coaster from lesson 4 and deliberately drop a stitch. Push it off the needle, pull the fabric sideways, and let it run down four or five rows. Then hook it back up. Doing this once, on something you do not care about, removes the panic entirely.
