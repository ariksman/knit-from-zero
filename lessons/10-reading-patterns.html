Reading patterns & charts

Knitting patterns are written in a compressed shorthand that looks like a cipher and takes about twenty minutes to learn. After this lesson you can read any pattern in any book, including the one at the end of this course.

25 minutes The last lesson before the project Worked examples

Why patterns are written this way

Because writing “knit two stitches, then purl two stitches, then repeat those four stitches all the way to the end of the round” eighty times would fill a book. The shorthand exists so that a whole garment fits on two pages, and so that a Finnish knitter and a Chilean one can use the same instructions.

“Rep from * to end” vs “rep from * to last n sts” The first means the repeat divides exactly into your stitch count. The second means it does not, and there is a leftover instruction at the end. This is why patterns insist on stitch counts that are “a multiple of 4” — a 2×2 rib needs the round to divide by 4 or the pattern will not meet itself.

Charts

A chart is a picture of the fabric, one square per stitch, seen from the right side. It is far easier to read than words once you know the three rules.

1 · Bottom to top

Row 1 is the bottom row of the chart, because that is the order in which the fabric is built.

2 · Right-side rows read right to left

The same direction the stitches travel across your needles. Row numbers on the right mark these.

3 · Wrong-side rows read left to right

…and every symbol is reversed: a blank square means purl on those rows. Row numbers on the left. Knitting in the round? Every round is a right-side round, so you always read right to left and symbols never reverse.

Sizes in brackets

Most published patterns cover several sizes at once, written as S (M, L, XL). Every number after that appears in the same order.

The trick every experienced knitter uses Before you cast on, print the pattern and go through it with a highlighter, marking only your size's numbers. Reading cast on 68 (76, 84, 92) sts correctly forty times in a row is a real source of errors, and the fix costs two minutes.

This project sidesteps the problem: the generator produces a pattern with exactly one number in each place, calculated for your dog.

How to read this pattern

The project pattern is laid out in seven numbered sections, each with a round range on the left and the instruction on the right:

2 · Widening over the chest

Rnds 25–45Work the increase round, then one plain knit round, alternately — a total of 11 increase rounds. 120 sts.
CheckThe piece measures about 7.0 cm below the collar.
  • The left column is either a round number range, or a keyword like Check, Note or Cast on. Round numbers run continuously from the very first round of the collar, so you can always tell where you are.
  • Bold numbers in the right column are stitch counts. Stop and count whenever you see one.
  • “Check” lines are measurements. Lay the work flat, unstretched, and measure. If you are more than a centimetre out, your row gauge differs from your swatch — follow the measurement, not the round count.
When rounds and centimetres disagree, believe the centimetres Row gauge is the least reliable number in knitting. Every “work until it measures X” instruction beats the round count sitting next to it. The one exception is the leg openings, where the back panel and the belly panel must have the same number of rows as each other — there, count.
