1 · The snood

A tube that sits over the head and covers the neck and ears. For a short-coated breed this is the single most useful cold-weather garment after the sweater — ears lose a surprising amount of heat, and a Basenji's are large, thin and permanently upright.

It is the collar of the sweater, made longer. If you can knit the collar you can knit this.

Pattern

Make it wearable both ways Knit it long enough that it can be pushed down as a neck warmer or pulled up over the ears. About 22 cm covers both uses on a Basenji. Bind off in pattern and very loosely — this edge goes over the widest part of the head.

2 · Leg warmers

Four small tubes. Genuinely useful on salted winter pavements, and the perfect first project for double-pointed needles or magic loop. Not every dog tolerates them; try one before knitting four.

Keep them loose at the top A tube that grips a leg above the joint restricts circulation. These should stay up through friction and ribbing, not compression. If one leaves a mark, it is too tight — go up four stitches.

3 · The boy-dog belly notch

Male dogs need the belly of the sweater to stop short. The generator has a switch for this; here is what it actually does.

How to work it: during the straight section, when the piece is about 4–6 cm short of where the hem rib would start, work to the belly, bind off the centre belly stitches (roughly 40% of the round), and work the remaining stitches back and forth. Change to the smaller needles for the last 5 cm and work in k2/p2 rib as before, then bind off loosely. Finally pick up along the notch edge and work three rounds of rib so it does not curl.

The sweater is now open underneath from the notch to the hem. It stays on because the collar, the chest and the leg openings do all the holding — the belly panel was never structural.

4 · A hi-vis stripe

Two rounds of fluorescent yarn across the back, worked into the straight section, turn the sweater into something visible from a car at dusk. It is the simplest possible colourwork: stop one yarn, start another, carry on.

How: at the round you want the stripe, simply start knitting with the new colour, leaving a 15 cm tail of each. Work two to four rounds, then swap back. Weave both tails in afterwards along the colour change so they do not show.

The jog: because a round is a spiral, the colour change will make a small step at the beginning of the round. Hide it by placing the beginning of the round at the belly, or by working the first stitch of the second round of the new colour together with the stitch below it.

Reflective yarn — nylon with a retroreflective filament plied in — is sold in most yarn shops for exactly this purpose and works even better than a bright colour.

5 · Things not to knit for a dog

IdeaWhy it seems goodWhy it is not
A hoodWarm ears, adorable photographs.It restricts hearing and peripheral vision, both of which a dog uses constantly. A snood does the same job without covering the eyes.
Cabled or lace panelsBeautiful.Every hole and every raised cable is somewhere a claw, a twig or a bramble can catch. Keep the outside of a working garment smooth.
Buttons or togglesEasy to put on.Chewable, swallowable, and they press into the dog when lying down. Use ribbing and stretch instead.
A long tail coverComplete coverage.A Basenji's tail curls tightly over the back and needs to move. Anything on it will be removed, thoroughly.
Pure alpacaExtremely warm and soft.It grows under its own weight, felts unpredictably, and sheds fibres the dog will inhale. Save it for your own hat.
A snug sweater for summer walksThey look cold in the wind.Dogs shed heat mainly through panting and their paws, but an insulating layer in warm weather is still a real overheating risk. Wool jumpers are for winter.
