3D models

Knitting is a three-dimensional structure pretending to be a flat one. Some things about it simply cannot be drawn: how loops pass through each other, why ribbing pulls in, why a stockinette swatch rolls up. Those are here, as real geometry you can turn over in your hands.

1 · One stitch, and its neighbours

The middle stitch is picked out in gold and the head of the stitch below it in blue. Turn the model until you are looking at it edge-on: you will see that the two legs of the gold stitch travel forward, towards you, while the loop they were pulled through sits behind them. That front-and-back relationship is the whole of knitting, and it is invisible in any flat diagram.

Compare it with the flat version in lesson 1.

2 · Every fabric in the course

The same generator, with only one thing changed: which way each stitch faces. Switch between them and turn the model over — garter looks identical from both sides, stockinette does not, and rib is the same fabric folded.

Try this · why ribbing grips Choose 2×2 rib and drag the relax the rib slider. No stitch changes size — the fabric simply folds, so the same width of knitting occupies less space. Let go of a real rib and exactly this happens. It is why the collar can go over a Basenji's head and then sit snugly on the neck.
Try this · why stockinette curls Choose Stockinette and drag let the swatch go. The top and bottom edges roll one way and the sides roll the other, because the loop is not symmetric front to back. This is why every edge in the sweater pattern is ribbing or garter, never plain stockinette.

3 · The dog, and the sweater on it

Built from the breed standard: body length exactly equal to height at the withers, because the Basenji is a square breed; depth of body 47% of that height; forearm 41.5%, which is what makes the dog look leggy. The ribcage cross-section is an upright oval nearly twice as deep as it is wide, and the waist is 65% of the chest girth.

The sweater is a second surface lofted over the same body, with the leg openings genuinely cut out of it. Turn the sections on and each colour is one numbered part of the pattern.

How these are made

The fabric

One continuous polyline is swept through every course of the swatch — because a piece of knitting is one continuous strand — and then thickened into a tube using parallel-transport frames so the yarn never twists as it turns.

The loop is a harmonic curve fitted to the classical textile models — Peirce's 1947 geometry of the plain knitted loop and Munden's 1959 relations — not something invented for the picture. Everything is in units of the yarn's own diameter: wales 4.5 diameters apart, courses 3.46, loop 1.5 course spacings tall so consecutive courses overlap by the amount they need to interlock at all, and the two legs pinching to exactly one diameter apart.

The property that makes it fabric rather than decoration is that depth crosses the mid-plane four times per stitch — head at the back, leg at the front, foot, sinker loop at the back again. Get that wrong and the loops stack instead of threading through one another; it looks like corduroy and a knitter will know instantly without being able to say why. The yarn is swept at half a diameter thick, which is the hard ceiling: adjacent needle-loop heads sit 1.1 diameters apart, so anything fatter would pass through itself.

The renderer

About four hundred lines of WebGL written for this site — no three.js, no libraries at all. One material: a hemisphere ambient term, a single directional light and a rim highlight, with the colours read from the same CSS custom properties as the rest of the page, so the models re-light themselves when you switch theme.

Nothing renders unless something changed, and nothing renders at all while the model is off screen.

If you see nothing above These need WebGL. If your browser has it disabled or your graphics driver is blocklisted you will get a short message instead of a model — and nothing is lost, because everything demonstrated here is also covered by the flat diagrams in lesson 1, lesson 5 and the measuring page.
